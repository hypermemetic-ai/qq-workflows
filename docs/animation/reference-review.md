# From first viewing to a played comparison

## Establish the motion

Watch the whole reference and the whole game take at their native pace, including the entry, recovery and next action. Write down the first difference you see before looking at joint errors. If your viewing surface only exposes stills, describe what you saw in the sequence and give the reviewer the full-speed film. A two-frame jump and a slow drift can have the same worst joint error.

Keep the original frame number, frame rate, player side and camera for each view. Label the actors by identity and the limbs by the actor's own left and right. When one passes in front of the other, pick a visible cue such as a jacket back, belt knot or shoe. Check it in the reverse view through the turn. A projected skeleton can draw a hidden wrist through a torso. Mark it as occluded and use a second view to resolve it when possible.

Keep the input or action clock when comparing timings. Use a second alignment for a question about shape. For example, compare a step on the press to see how late it starts, then align both takes on the first foot lift to see whether the body actually dips as far. Label the offset in frames or milliseconds. With different cameras, compare silhouettes, facing and the order of events. Pixel offsets need a measured registration.

## Revise a passage

Pick the first passage where the motion stops making sense. Read what both actors are doing before, during and after it. In a turning step, the chest can face the camera while the pelvis and planted shoe still face across the stage. A facing check on the chest alone can pass the wrong drawing. For a handoff, an artist may need to change both actors if the receiver never comes within reach on the agreed clock.

Use the tool that can distinguish the plausible causes. A fixed wide view and a visible floor mark distinguish a planted sole from a camera pan. A view along the travel line shows motion in depth. Playing the rig with and without the garment separates a folded panel from a twisted arm. Check marks against the unannotated picture. Use strips with stable frame labels for fast passages, then give the edit its lead-in and follow-through again.

Edit the source that produces the played pose. Keep the base take recoverable. Replay with the same inputs and framing after each meaningful change. If game timing or world travel forces a different performance, show the concrete tradeoff while it is still cheap to choose. A character who has to retreat two metres will give a different impression from one who can stand where they landed.

## Decide what to show

Lead with a short, full-speed comparison from the ordinary camera. Match the event clock, keep both bodies and feet visible where possible, identify each panel, and offer the opposite view when one actor hides the other. A still or a slow, numbered strip can support a specific finding. Give the owner a film they can open on their device.

Check the first action after control returns, especially a walk or turn from a held pose. Exercise the other player slot and a neighboring route that shares the drawing. Report which comparison you actually watched, where the evidence is weak and what the owner is being asked to decide. A measured change earns its keep when someone can see the intended improvement in play.
